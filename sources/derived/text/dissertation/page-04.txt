Cairo’s new developments continue to take from modern aesthetic languages, mainly 
within New Cairo and the New Administrative Capital. There the aesthetics are inspired 
from those big cities, symbols of global ideals while it often overlooks the site specific 
design like climate, social behaviour, and history as it is not specific to Cairo. As 
architecture becomes more separated from culture questions rise, where is individuality? 
Where did architecture’s ability to tell stories go? And what happens to identity when 
different cities all begin to look the same? 
Historically, Cairo’s architecture was shaped by climate, social life, and natural changes. In 
the past few decades, this has leaned toward real estate driven developments and large 
scale planning that prioritise quick construction, aesthetic appeal, and commercial return, 
often producing spaces that are disconnected from Cairo’s history. The research puts 
homogenisation under the light as not just a phenomenon, but as a social and spatial 
concept that is controlled by economic pressures, government decisions, and developers’ 
ideals. 
Using Cairo as the main focus, the selected case studies being researched are the New 
Administrative Capital, a central district called Zamalek, and another called West el Balad 
and finally the informal neighbourhoods called Ashwa’iyat. The research looks at theories of 
globalisation, critical regionalism, and phenomenology of place. Through literature review, 
urban analysis, analytical drawings, and interviews with architects, it explores how 
homogenisation is accomplished. The dissertation dives into whether or not the future of 
Cairo’s architectural identity is at risk and how it depends on balancing contemporary 
development with culture and context responsive design as well as understanding 
architecture’s role as a story teller of history.
