global cities, to developing areas, hence creating this inconsistent power dynamic in spatial 
design (King). Architectural models created in Western contexts are replicated elsewhere, 
usually without any amending to local culture or climate. Yet again contributing to 
homogenisation by embracing the universal aesthetic symbolized by success and 
development (King). 
In upcoming cities like Cairo, globalization comes around with goals like economic growth 
and international recognition. In order to show modern identity they opt for large scale 
developments especially in times of increased tourism and cultural investment (Sassen). 
However, this focus on attracting tourists and being recognized worldwide often leads to 
pushing away from the existing history that lives in the day to day designs. Over time this 
will replace the local identity causing Cairo to adopt another generic design (King). 
Furthermore, homogenisation is not just style, but an outcome of chasing capital and urban 
production. 
3.3 Critical Regionalism and Contextual Design 
Responding to the homogenising effects of global modernism, Frampton presents ‘Critical 
Regionalism’ as an architectural approach that blends both universal civilisation and local 
culture (Frampton). Frampton does not go against modernity, but supports it in a different 
light. He believes in architecture that focuses on climate, topography, materiality, and 
cultural context. He says the way in which to fight homogenization is through feel, social 
awareness, and spatial orientation. (Frampton). 
This theory shows that architecture should come from site specific context instead of 
copying styles. Critical regionalism has a theory of how to reject generic styles. Doing this 
by sourcing local materials, design climate specific approaches, and really focus on cultural 
design, (Frampton).
