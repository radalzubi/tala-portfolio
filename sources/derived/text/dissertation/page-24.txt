 
 
Zamalek and West El Balad is a slower form of homogenisation, where it is fading slowly 
through small commercial renovations. Façade changes and repetitive designs adopted by 
economic return slowly weakening historical presence. Analytical drawings show how 
these areas keep their historic touch, however the pressures of modernisation make these 
qualities seem less important. Going back to the architects making observations that 
architectural loss often happens through market research change rather than intentional 
elimination. 
In contrast, the Ashwa’iyat show a parallel urban setting that is based on informality, needs, 
and community engagement. These neighbourhoods aren’t necessarily stable but still show 
deep meaningful social interactions and cultural presence. Architectural identity lives 
through them, the artwork, the constant additions in construction, and everyday routines. 
These settings question the common belief that informality leads to ‘failure’ and instead 
show how these cities can embrace and learn from these ‘failures’.  
The interviews strengthened the findings by showing the true way homogenisation works. 
The architects discussed how developer led designs and cost effective decisions were the 
key constant factors. This causes environments to focus on marketing and speed. At the 
same time, architects looked to methods to resist this like adaptive reuse, local material 
choices, and attention to experience. These methods truly show that architects can still 
make their independent decisions without crossing the line over into the developers 
requests. 
The phenomenology aspect of architecture is the key for understanding what is lost 
through homogenisation. Beyond façades and styles, identity is born in atmosphere, rituals, 
and interactions. Hospitality, street life, and artistic interventions are just a few examples 
that highlight architectural meaning in an experience, not just a façade. When cities 
renovate and choose modern designs from bigger cities, they don’t just risk losing the 
unique look and identity of their own country but also lose a deep and emotional 
connection. What is architecture without it?
