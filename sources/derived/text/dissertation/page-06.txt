 
 
Architectural homogenisation is the increase of visual and spatial similarity of cities across 
the world. Different demographics all driven by the same forces. Koolhaas best captures 
this through his work, the “Generic City,” describing urban environments that become 
detached from history, identity, and site specific context over time (Koolhaas). Within the 
Generic City, architecture prioritises efficiency, flexibility, and scale, creating landscapes 
that would be overtaken by glass towers, neutral palettes, and repetitive masterplans 
(Koolhaas). 
Koolhaas argues that this comes from processes of globalisation and capital flow, allowing 
urban forms to be replicated across the world with little to no change (Koolhaas). As cities 
compete for global recognition, architecture becomes repetitive. This is due to the fact that 
it is no longer influenced and driven by culture but more by efficiency and market 
demands. This makes environments that feel as though they don’t belong anywhere, lost in 
the world with no identity and no need for change. 
These patterns are becoming more visible in Cairo because of the increase in gated 
communities and large scale developments like the New Administrative Capital. Such 
projects often use international design languages that focus on how to make things look 
modern while ignoring climate, social experience, and history. Koolhaas’s framework 
furthermore gives a perspective for understanding how Cairo’s contemporary development 
adds to a wider range of global trends toward homogenisation (Koolhaas). 
3.2 Globalisation, Capital, and Urban Production 
Building on Koolhaas’s philosophy, Sassen, the theorist of ‘Global City’, stresses how the 
global economic networks shape modern cities. They identify global cities as strategic 
nodes in international financial systems where architecture is used to attract investment, 
tourists, and business (Sassen). Urban form is becoming more and more influenced by 
market logic, creating landmark projects and business districts to show that the city is 
competing to this ideal of constant renovation (Sassen).
