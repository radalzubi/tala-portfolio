Homogenisation of Cairo’s Urban Horizons 
Cultural Identity, Lived Experience, and Contemporary Urban Practice​
 
Abstract  
This dissertation investigates how the growing homogenisation of the contemporary built 
environment of Cairo impacts its own architectural identity. Through a mix of different 
methods like a literature review, analytical urban drawings, critical observation, and semi 
structured interviews with practicing architects in Egypt, this research examines how 
homogenisation rises across multiple urban scales and how architects and communities 
deal with these pressures in practice. 
The study is structured by three theoretical perspectives. First the perspective on 
globalisation and the Generic City through the lens’ of Koolhaas, Sassen and King,  followed 
by critical regionalism through Frampton, and finishing off with phenomenology of place 
with Norberg Schulz, Pallasmaa and Zumthor. Together this establishes an analytical lens 
that is used to assess Cairo’s evolving urban state. The case studies included are the New 
Administrative Capital of Cairo and different scaled districts within central Cairo to 
compare between large scale development plans, historical urban identity, and 
neighbourhoods that gradually changed through everyday life. Simultaneously revealing 
alternative approaches rooted in adaptive reuse, context specific responsiveness, and 
focused strategies. The research sheds light on ways to resist homogenisation and 
negotiate terms that reinforce Cairo’s character.

01 Introduction  
We live in an age defined by speed. Architecture today is shaped by efficiency, refinement, 
and visual simplicity with sleek façades, smooth surfaces, and quick construction methods 
that prioritise functionality and time efficiency. Buildings rise quickly, cities expand even 
quicker, and design begins to respond to convenience rather than continuity now more 
than ever. Contemporary architecture embraces clean modernism, technological 
advancement, and global unity. Yet in this pursuit of progress, something quieter has begun 
to fade. 
There was a time when architecture was crafted slowly and deliberately. There was a 
purpose. Corners were engraved, materials were layered, and façades had the work of a 
lifetime. Stone was carved through generations, details told stories, and buildings were 
made with intricate detailing, focus and patience rather than speed and profit turnover. 
Inconvenience was not avoided but embraced. The thick walls to control climate, the 
courtyards for social interactions, and the architectural detail for cultural values. 
Architecture was not just built, it was embraced and taken care of. 
Today, skylines all over Dubai, London, New York, and even Cairo, are beginning to look the 
same. Glass towers, muted palettes, and generic masterplans take over these cities, cities 
that are often seen as references of modernity and progress. As they become the 
benchmarks of finance, culture, and development, they become reference points for what 
contemporary architecture should look like, acting as a muse and model for emerging 
cities. This creates environments that feel generic no matter the place, this is 
homogenisation.  
Cairo today exists between two realities. One side, shaped by centuries of history, climate 
responsive architecture, and social life. On the other side lies New Cairo with spaces that 
are defined by large scale masterplans, gated communities, and modern developments that 
are meant to symbolize Cairo as a city of progress, modernisation, and goals. Egypt is an 
emerging global destination, especially since the opening of the Grand Egyptian Museum

and tourism rates have gone up. Architecture has become a visible tool for projecting their 
advancements as a country to the world. The country feels the need to be seen as 
contemporary, efficient, and internationally up to date to prove their growth.  
Cairo’s new developments continue to take from modern aesthetic languages, mainly 
within New Cairo and the New Administrative Capital. There the aesthetics are inspired 
from those big cities, symbols of global ideals while it often overlooks the site specific 
design like climate, social behaviour, and history as it is not specific to Cairo. As 
architecture becomes more separated from culture questions rise, where is individuality? 
Where did architecture’s ability to tell stories go? And what happens to identity when 
different cities all begin to look the same? 
Historically, Cairo’s architecture was shaped by climate, social life, and natural changes. In 
the past few decades, this has leaned toward real estate driven developments and large 
scale planning that prioritise quick construction, aesthetic appeal, and commercial return, 
often producing spaces that are disconnected from Cairo’s history. The research puts 
homogenisation under the light as not just a phenomenon, but as a social and spatial 
concept that is controlled by economic pressures, government decisions, and developers’ 
ideals. 
Using Cairo as the main focus, the selected case studies being researched are the New 
Administrative Capital, a central district called Zamalek, and another called West el Balad 
and finally the informal neighbourhoods called Ashwa’iyat. The research looks at theories of 
globalisation, critical regionalism, and phenomenology of place. Through literature review, 
urban analysis, analytical drawings, and interviews with architects, it explores how 
homogenisation is accomplished. The dissertation dives into whether or not the future of 
Cairo’s architectural identity is at risk and how it depends on balancing contemporary 
development with culture and context responsive design as well as understanding 
architecture’s role as a story teller of history.

02 Positionality  
This research is informed by my position as an Egyptian British architecture student who 
has lived between Cairo and London. Experiencing both worlds has made me understand 
questions of architectural identity, cultural unity, and globalised development. Growing up 
in Cairo allowed for firsthand experience of historic urban architecture in Zamalek and 
West el Balad as well as the Ashwa’iyat. While studying in the UK I got insight into the 
planning designs and contemporary architectural practices of the West. These experiences 
eventually influence my perception of architecture and my interest in homogenisation as 
well as becoming visually aware of its impact. 
I am aware that this background may push me to value culture over homogenisation. To 
limit this personal bias, this dissertation adopts a comparative and evidence based 
methodology, combining architectural theories, urban analysis, analytical drawings, and 
semi structured interviews with practicing architects. Rather than presenting 
homogenisation as a negative aspect, the research approaches it as a social and contextual 
process controlled by economic pressure, government decisions, and practices. 
By gaining information through semi structured interviews, researched case studies, and 
analytical drawings by urban observation, the research is balanced and subjective with 
critical analysis. This reflective approach allows architectural identity to be examined not 
as something set, but as something constantly changing by both global influence and local 
practice. 
 
03 Literature Review  
3.1 Architectural Homogenisation and the Generic City 
4 

=== PAGE 6 ===
 
 
Architectural homogenisation is the increase of visual and spatial similarity of cities across 
the world. Different demographics all driven by the same forces. Koolhaas best captures 
this through his work, the “Generic City,” describing urban environments that become 
detached from history, identity, and site specific context over time (Koolhaas). Within the 
Generic City, architecture prioritises efficiency, flexibility, and scale, creating landscapes 
that would be overtaken by glass towers, neutral palettes, and repetitive masterplans 
(Koolhaas). 
Koolhaas argues that this comes from processes of globalisation and capital flow, allowing 
urban forms to be replicated across the world with little to no change (Koolhaas). As cities 
compete for global recognition, architecture becomes repetitive. This is due to the fact that 
it is no longer influenced and driven by culture but more by efficiency and market 
demands. This makes environments that feel as though they don’t belong anywhere, lost in 
the world with no identity and no need for change. 
These patterns are becoming more visible in Cairo because of the increase in gated 
communities and large scale developments like the New Administrative Capital. Such 
projects often use international design languages that focus on how to make things look 
modern while ignoring climate, social experience, and history. Koolhaas’s framework 
furthermore gives a perspective for understanding how Cairo’s contemporary development 
adds to a wider range of global trends toward homogenisation (Koolhaas). 
3.2 Globalisation, Capital, and Urban Production 
Building on Koolhaas’s philosophy, Sassen, the theorist of ‘Global City’, stresses how the 
global economic networks shape modern cities. They identify global cities as strategic 
nodes in international financial systems where architecture is used to attract investment, 
tourists, and business (Sassen). Urban form is becoming more and more influenced by 
market logic, creating landmark projects and business districts to show that the city is 
competing to this ideal of constant renovation (Sassen). 
5 

=== PAGE 7 ===
 
 
Anthony D. King explains that architectural modernity is usually adopted from dominant 
global cities, to developing areas, hence creating this inconsistent power dynamic in spatial 
design (King). Architectural models created in Western contexts are replicated elsewhere, 
usually without any amending to local culture or climate. Yet again contributing to 
homogenisation by embracing the universal aesthetic symbolized by success and 
development (King). 
In upcoming cities like Cairo, globalization comes around with goals like economic growth 
and international recognition. In order to show modern identity they opt for large scale 
developments especially in times of increased tourism and cultural investment (Sassen). 
However, this focus on attracting tourists and being recognized worldwide often leads to 
pushing away from the existing history that lives in the day to day designs. Over time this 
will replace the local identity causing Cairo to adopt another generic design (King). 
Furthermore, homogenisation is not just style, but an outcome of chasing capital and urban 
production. 
3.3 Critical Regionalism and Contextual Design 
Responding to the homogenising effects of global modernism, Frampton presents ‘Critical 
Regionalism’ as an architectural approach that blends both universal civilisation and local 
culture (Frampton). Frampton does not go against modernity, but supports it in a different 
light. He believes in architecture that focuses on climate, topography, materiality, and 
cultural context. He says the way in which to fight homogenization is through feel, social 
awareness, and spatial orientation. (Frampton). 
This theory shows that architecture should come from site specific context instead of 
copying styles. Critical regionalism has a theory of how to reject generic styles. Doing this 
by sourcing local materials, design climate specific approaches, and really focus on cultural 
design, (Frampton). 
The shaded streets, courtyards and thick walls in Cairo are all historical architectural 
examples of traditions that answer to the climate and social life there. Contemporary 
6 

=== PAGE 8 ===
 
 
designs that choose these strategies show that context and site specific design are still 
important. Frampton’s work acts as a lens for analyzing if recent developments in Cairo 
respond purposefully with place or just adopt generic global design(Frampton). 
3.4 Phenomenology of Place and Lived Experience 
Critical regionalism pays attention to architectural form and environment, phenomenon 
theorists put their attention towards lived and sensory experience. Norberg Schulz says 
that architecture’s role is to create meaningful connections between people and place to 
encourage human engagements. This is believed to be done through introducing the 
concept of ‘Genius Loci', or the spirit of place (Norberg Schulz). Architecture is special by 
being able to create an experience inside a place (Norberg Schulz). 
Similarly, Pallasmaa believes in multisensory design and that architecture must have touch, 
feel, memory, and atmosphere (Pallasmaa). Zumthor shares that thought when he stressed 
the importance of material presence and emotional impact, saying that architecture must 
have meaning through sense and experience (Zumthor). 
Phenomenology changes the meaning of homogenisation, it is an experience of how spaces 
are felt, occupied, and remembered (Norberg Schulz, Pallasmaa, Zumthor). This new 
meaning of it lets architectural identity be seen in a different light, as a phenomenon that is 
constantly changing, never fixed or still. 
3.5 Egyptian Perspectives on Architecture Identity 
Egyptian architecture philosophies further elaborate on these global theories. Hassan Fathy 
speaks for climate responsive and community led architecture. While he stresses the 
importance of cultural knowledge, local materials, and socially embedded design, his work 
shows how architectural identity in Egypt historically changed (Fathy). These changes are 
because of needs, environment, and community life rather than to keep up with the global 
trends (Fathy). 
7 

=== PAGE 9 ===
 
 
A more recent architect, Walid Arafa, continues this theory by combining contemporary 
design with culture. This shows how modern architecture can remain in place while still 
meeting today’s needs (Arafa). These perspectives stress on the importance that 
architectural identity in Egypt is another factor that is always changing. Whether it is 
through adaptation, reuse, or lived experiences, it is never fixed.  
These local perspectives support the argument that Cairo’s architectural identity has 
changed through community, climate response, and history. However, today’s 
developments prioritize speed and marketability. By putting both perspectives together, 
this dissertation places Cairo in a broader discussion on homogenisation while analyzing its 
cultural and historical conditions. 
 
04 Methodology 
4.1 Research Approach and Case Study Selection 
The research is a comparative urban analysis across different scales, analysing both large 
scale developments and small everyday neighbourhoods. Three key case studies are the 
New Administrative Capital, representing large scale state and developer decision driven 
expansion, central Cairo districts Zamalek and West El Balad, reflecting historic urban 
memory with bits of contemporary pressure, and Ashwa’iyat (informal neighbourhoods), 
which demonstrate adaptation and community driven spatial design that organically grown 
from the lower class. 
These case studies were chosen to embody the contrasting natures of urban designs in 
Egypt. The planned versus nature, formal versus informal, international versus local design. 
Side by side, comparing and contrasting, they provide a comprehensive research which 
through that homogenisation can be seen as a spatial, social, and cultural concept. 
4.2 Analytical Urban Drawings and Observation 
8

Analytical urban drawings create a main factor of the research that serves as tools for 
significant and individual interpretation rather than representational and generic imagery. 
Mapping, sectional studies, and diagrammatic analysis were used to examine urban forms, 
shapes and structures as well as circulation patterns, material presence, and significant 
relationships across the selected case study areas. These drawings were curated by direct 
observation and photographic documentation. 
The urban observation part focused on street life, transitions between public and private 
space, climatic response and structure detailings. Particular attention was given to the 
detailings and intricate work done which revealed contrasts between highly controlled 
developments and organically evolved neighbourhoods. Attention to how people moved 
and viewed the developments were also analyzed. This visual and use of space methodology 
allows homogenisation to be understood not only through form, but through function. 
4.3 Semi-Structured Interviews 
Semi structured interviews were conducted with well known practising architects working 
within the Cairo context. The architects practice in different fields allowing for more depth 
to be covered. This method was selected to allow for the discussion to be structured while 
still having free flowing expansion on professional experiences and personal perspectives. 
Interview questions focused on perceptions of architectural homogenisation, the loss of 
identity (if there was), and ways to bring it back through contextual and socially focused 
practice. 
The interviews were transcribed and analysed to identify the root issue of homogenisation. 
Also used to depict any underlying patterns relating to market and development, any focus 
on climate responsiveness architecture, adaptive reuse, public life, and informal urban 
architecture. These professional insights complement the urban analysis by revealing the 
realities shaping Cairo’s built environment.

05 Urban Analysis - Case Study 
This urban analysis looks at how architectural homogenisation shows up in different urban 
conditions throughout Cairo. The four main studies previously mentioned, the New 
Administrative Capital, Zamalek, West El Balad, and Ashwa’iyat. They are a contrasting 
range of areas. Using analytical urban drawings, site observation, and photographic 
documentation, this explores spatial, material, social, and climate responsiveness. This 
shows modern developments’ relationship with history and informal urban practices.

The New Administrative Capital represents the most accurate representation of state led 
and developer driven growth in Cairo. Its goal was to be a symbol of progress, growth and 
modernisation, publicized by its large scale, wide boulevards, and government areas. 
Analytical drawings demonstrate a spatial logic behind it through the isolated buildings and 
how it prioritizes vehicle movement over pedestrians, visual symmetry over social 
environments, and spatial organization over human engagement.

5.4 Summary of Findings 
Across all four studies, homogenisation comes out as a complex process. It is controlled by 
economic ambition, developer control, and foreign architectural styles. The New 
Administrative Capital reflects large scale generic modernization that only prioritizes visual 
modernity over human experience. Zamalek and West El Balad reflect how historic 
neighborhoods slowly lose their character through profit driven renovation. Ashwa’iyat, 
however, reveals different ways of thinking spatially, socially and fluidly.  
These case studies demonstrate that architectural identity in Cairo is not vanishing 
overtime, but is being renovated bit by bit. While formal developments adopt globalised 
aesthetics thinking they are improving the city, informal neighbourhoods and adaptive 
historic concepts continue to defend their culturally rich environment.  
 
06 Architects Perspective on Homogenisation in Cairo 
The purpose of the interviews with the architects was to understand how practicing 
architects perceive the rise of architectural homogenisation in their city. The purpose was 
also to understand the driving forces as well as the interference it has with cultural identity 
and experience. The following analysis will integrate the recurring statements and specific 
quotes from the architects and place them within debates on globalisation, urban memory, 
and design practice discovered from previous theories and philosophies researched.  
After the interviews were recorded there were five recurring themes that all architects 
touched base on. First the developer led design and logical thinking for the market, second, 
division of architectural identity and the rise of social life, third, environmental disregard 
18 

=== PAGE 20 ===
 
 
and loss of climate responsive architecture, fourth, resistance of adaptive reuse and 
context responsive design, and lastly informal practices as carriers of urban identity. These 
themes reveal not only shared concerns but also a variety of priorities based on the 
architects’ professional experiences and design philosophies. 
6.1 Developer Led Urbanism and Logical Thinking 
The most dominant theme across all interviews was the perception that market centered 
logic and developer control were central drivers of homogenisation in Cairo. According to 
Salem Mostafa, current development models prioritise visual appeal, branding, and 
financial efficiency over climatic responsive design, cultural continuity, and contextual 
design: “Architectural decisions are often driven by financial efficiency and visual appeal 
rather than spatial quality or cultural unity.” (Mostafa) 
Similar to this, Hisham Ghorab said that there was a priority of commercial return over 
protecting architecture in historic neighbourhoods. Examples are West El Balad and 
Zamalek, where renovations are made in favour of generic global styles: “Many streets have 
lost their specificity, replaced by generic storefronts and repetitive alterations aimed at fast 
commercial return rather than cultural continuity.” (Ghorab) 
These statements reflect broader academic observations about globalisation’s influence on 
architectural production. This world is economically dependent, the commercial return 
often overrides identity embedded design (Sassen and King). The architects strongly stood 
on the fact that the developers (often the ones paying the architects) priorities not just 
aesthetics but also space orientation which leads the environments to resemble one 
another on both the inside and the outside across different sites and cities. 
6.2 Breaking Apart Architectural Identity 
Architects also stated that homogenisation in Cairo is not only superficial style and goes 
into the social life and real life experiences. Salem Mostafa highlighted how there is a 
decline of public space and street life, arguing that it is due to private compounds and 
19 

=== PAGE 21 ===
 
 
gated developments that caused a divide in the social unity: “Public space is steadily 
diminishing… reducing opportunities for spontaneous social interaction.”(Mostafa) This 
aligns with Leithy’s opinion of contemporary urbanisation, where historical presence has 
been replaced by broken up and disconnected developments: “Architecture has shifted 
toward speed, density, and surface-level modernity … weakening the urban character that 
once defined Cairo.” (Leithy)  
For Nehal Lehta, architectural identity is not carried in just preserved façades but is 
experienced through everyday life. She believed that West El Balad’s ongoing renovations 
should prioritise and embrace the social life and architecture rather than be like a museum 
and keep them as is in preservation: “Identity is not just heritage façades but the daily life 
they contain.” (Lehta) These inputs almost copy or mimic Norberg Schulz’s philosophy that 
true architecture is in the human interaction with space and place (Norberg Schulz), once 
again stressing that identity loss cannot be measured just by viewing the architecture but 
must truly experience the social terms of it. 
6.3 Loss of Environmental and Climate Responsiveness 
Several architects saw another aspect of homogenisation, how the detachment of 
contemporary architecture from environmental thinking and climate response was 
increasing. Salem Mostafa said the usage of sealed glass buildings disregard traditional 
simple solutions like shading, courtyards, and local material:“New projects rarely draw from 
traditional principles such as shaded circulation, passive cooling strategies, or locally 
rooted material use.” (Mostafa) 
Leithy emphasised that Cairo’s historical architecture, that integrated climate responsive 
design through form and material, has been ignored by designs that prioritise visual 
modernity over environmental logic:“Elements such as minarets, courtyards, arcades 
shared climate intelligence across eras. Today, this dialogue has fractured.” (Leithy) 
This opinion goes along the same focus of Frampton, architecture’s responsibility to 
respond to the local environment (Frampton). The neglect of climate responsive 
20 

=== PAGE 22 ===
 
 
architecture proves that this universal homogenous language often ignores site specific 
design. 
6.4 Resisting Homogenisation through Adaptive Reuse and Context Specificity  
Yes there is a widespread concern about homogenisation across the interviews however 
the architects also claimed that there are design strategies that resist this by focusing 
solely on contextualism, adaptive reuse, and human focused spatial design. Hisham Ghorab 
spoke on projects like Emaret Kodak and Consulya, where historic exteriors are preserved 
while interiors are adapted to contemporary use: “Façades, proportions, and historic details 
must stay… interiors can adapt to modern needs.” (Ghorab) This approach shows just how 
heritage structures can be renovated without completely erasing their identity. Showing 
that preservation and modern adaptation are not incompatible and can in fact co-exist. 
Salem Mostafa also similarly described how his own practice is driven by site conditions, 
climatic response, and human behaviour. This just emphasises his careful attention to 
circulation, transitions between indoor and outdoor spaces, and spatial experience: “I try 
to design spaces that encourage movement, interaction, and a sense of belonging.” 
(Mostafa) 
All these factors and strategies align with Critical Regionalism, which prioritize the use of 
local materials, environmental integration, and site specific design as a way to fend off 
homogenisation in contemporary architecture (Frampton). 
6.5 Informal Practices as Carriers of Urban Identity 
A unique contribution of these interviews that stood out most was the focus on informal 
urban practices and community led identity, the Ashwa’iyat. Nehal Lehta is the one 
architect who highlighted Ashwa’iyat. They are simply neighbourhoods not designed as 
failed attempts but as sites of organic identity where residents choose to live, central to 
cairo and near the highways. Over the years the residents have been sought to be bought 
out by bigger developments however they choose to resist. This inspired artists collaborate 
21 

=== PAGE 23 ===
 
 
with the residents and transform the Ashwa’iyat into façades with large scale murals that 
reflect collective narratives: “Murals turned walls into collective art, revealing identity 
through colour, poetry, and context.” (Lehta) 
She also discussed the preservation of movements and experience beyond physical form, 
like the continued ritual of hospitality in Mohamed Saghayer’s salon, which retains an 
architectural ethos of care, history and presence: “Keeping these core experiences alive is 
as important as physical preservation.”(Lehta) 
This perspective goes beyond just understanding architectural identity. She sheds light on 
how it is crucial to include everyday practices, rituals, and informal creations. Such a 
community driven concept is so much more impactful than any other resistance. It shows a 
greater resistance to homogenisation that comes from inside the community which only 
deepens the value of lived and experienced culture even more. 
 
07 Discussion 
This dissertation was aimed to analyze how architectural homogenisation is changing 
Cairo’s built environment and what that means for cultural identity. Through literature 
reviews, urban analysis, analytical drawings, and architect interviews, homogenisation can 
be seen as a social and spatial concept focusing on profit, developer led decisions, and 
foreign aesthetics of architectural modernity. 
The Urban Analysis shows homogenisation scattered in all areas around Cairo. The New 
Administrative Capital is the most accurate example of generic homogenisation, a large 
scale of repetitive isolated building prioritizing image, convenience, and what will be seen 
as progress over lived experience. It's the spatial logic that truly exposes how that the city 
was designed mainly for show rather than  actual everyday life. This goes along with the 
theory of Koolhaas’ ‘Generic City’, where architecture becomes constantly changing and 
disconnected from community, history, and climate. 
22 

=== PAGE 24 ===
 
 
Zamalek and West El Balad is a slower form of homogenisation, where it is fading slowly 
through small commercial renovations. Façade changes and repetitive designs adopted by 
economic return slowly weakening historical presence. Analytical drawings show how 
these areas keep their historic touch, however the pressures of modernisation make these 
qualities seem less important. Going back to the architects making observations that 
architectural loss often happens through market research change rather than intentional 
elimination. 
In contrast, the Ashwa’iyat show a parallel urban setting that is based on informality, needs, 
and community engagement. These neighbourhoods aren’t necessarily stable but still show 
deep meaningful social interactions and cultural presence. Architectural identity lives 
through them, the artwork, the constant additions in construction, and everyday routines. 
These settings question the common belief that informality leads to ‘failure’ and instead 
show how these cities can embrace and learn from these ‘failures’.  
The interviews strengthened the findings by showing the true way homogenisation works. 
The architects discussed how developer led designs and cost effective decisions were the 
key constant factors. This causes environments to focus on marketing and speed. At the 
same time, architects looked to methods to resist this like adaptive reuse, local material 
choices, and attention to experience. These methods truly show that architects can still 
make their independent decisions without crossing the line over into the developers 
requests. 
The phenomenology aspect of architecture is the key for understanding what is lost 
through homogenisation. Beyond façades and styles, identity is born in atmosphere, rituals, 
and interactions. Hospitality, street life, and artistic interventions are just a few examples 
that highlight architectural meaning in an experience, not just a façade. When cities 
renovate and choose modern designs from bigger cities, they don’t just risk losing the 
unique look and identity of their own country but also lose a deep and emotional 
connection. What is architecture without it? 
23 

=== PAGE 25 ===
 
 
Cairo is not just a city where global modern trends come and go, but as a city where 
identity is constantly changing and negotiated. 
 
08 Conclusion 
While Cairo’s horizons continue to homogenise like those big cities around, the outcome 
may not be so tragic. Today’s skylines may become tomorrow’s memory, therefore creating 
entirely new attachments and cultural meanings. Homogenisation does change Cairo and 
sometimes in a repetitive manner, however through this research of the local practices and 
community engagement possible alternate futures for Cairo’s architectural identity arose. 
Therefore, homogenisation is not a final outcome, but an ongoing back and forth 
discussion. Merely a negotiation between modernisation and memory.  Cairo has the 
opportunity to change repetition into relevance and homogenisation into shared identity.  
Homogenisation is always on the horizon, yet its impact lies on how it is transformed.
