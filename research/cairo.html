Research · Beyond the Generic City

Homogenisation of Cairo’s Urban Horizons

Cultural Identity, Lived Experience, and Contemporary Urban Practice

Dissertation
University of Reading, School of Architecture, 2025–26
Length
5,039 words

01

The question

Is the future of Cairo’s architectural identity at risk, and how does it depend on balancing contemporary development with culture and context-responsive design? The dissertation treats homogenisation not simply as a style but as a social and spatial process shaped by economic pressure, government decisions and developers’ priorities.

Source: Dissertation, p.4, 5

This dissertation investigates how the growing homogenisation of the contemporary built environment of Cairo impacts its own architectural identity. Through a mix of different methods like a literature review, analytical urban drawings, critical observation, and semi structured interviews with practicing architects in Egypt, this research examines how homogenisation rises across multiple urban scales and how architects and communities deal with these pressures in practice.

Dissertation, p.1 (abstract)

02

Lenses and methods

Three theoretical perspectives and an Egyptian counterpoint frame a comparative urban analysis across scales.

  • Globalisation and the Generic City

    Koolhaas · Sassen · King

    Koolhaas’s Generic City describes urban environments that become detached from history, identity and site-specific context, prioritising efficiency, flexibility and scale. Sassen reads cities as nodes in global financial networks where architecture attracts investment, and King shows how models made in dominant cities are replicated elsewhere with little amendment to local culture or climate.

    Source: Dissertation, p.6, 7

  • Critical regionalism

    Frampton

    Frampton’s critical regionalism does not reject modernity but asks architecture to answer climate, topography, materiality and cultural context. The dissertation uses it as a lens for whether recent developments in Cairo respond purposefully to place or simply adopt generic global design.

    Source: Dissertation, p.7, 8

  • Phenomenology of place

    Norberg-Schulz · Pallasmaa · Zumthor

    Through genius loci, multisensory design and material presence, phenomenology reframes homogenisation as an experience of how spaces are felt, occupied and remembered, so that architectural identity is read as constantly changing rather than fixed.

    Source: Dissertation, p.8

  • Egyptian perspectives

    Hassan Fathy · Walid Arafa

    Fathy’s climate-responsive, community-led architecture and Arafa’s combination of contemporary design with culture show architectural identity in Egypt as something that has always changed through needs, environment and community life rather than global trends.

    Source: Dissertation, p.8, 9

  • Comparative case studies across scales

    Large-scale, state- and developer-led expansion (the New Administrative Capital); central districts carrying historic urban memory under contemporary pressure (Zamalek and West el Balad); and informal neighbourhoods that have grown organically through community-driven adaptation (Ashwa’iyat). The cases embody contrasting conditions: planned versus organic, formal versus informal, international versus local.

    Source: Dissertation, p.9

  • Analytical urban drawings and observation

    Mapping, sectional studies and diagrammatic analysis of urban forms, circulation, material presence and relationships, curated from direct observation and photographic documentation. Observation concentrated on street life, transitions between public and private space, climatic response and detailing.

    Source: Dissertation, p.10

  • Semi-structured interviews

    Interviews with practising architects working in the Cairo context, transcribed and analysed for patterns relating to market and development, climate responsiveness, adaptive reuse, public life and informal urban architecture.

    Source: Dissertation, p.10

Theme

Case 1 of 4

New Administrative Capital

Large-scale, state-led and developer-driven expansion — a new city of wide boulevards, isolated buildings and government districts.

The New Administrative Capital is read as the clearest example of generic homogenisation: a symbol of progress built from glass façades, neutral material schemes and repetitive office and residential blocks that resemble districts in London, Dubai or New York. The drawings show a spatial logic that prioritises vehicle movement over pedestrians, visual symmetry over social environments and spatial organisation over human engagement.

Observations

Built form
Most buildings are near-identical: glass façades, neutral palettes and generic office and residential blocks. Dissertation, p.13
Street life
Little or no public space, there mainly for show rather than as a social environment — unlike the always-busy street life of the rest of Cairo. Dissertation, p.14
Climate response
The adopted style is not made for Cairo’s climate; it relies on mechanical cooling instead of shading, courtyards and thick walls. Dissertation, p.13
Identity
A core example of Koolhaas’s Generic City: identity is replaced by bigger, modern designs detached from site specificity. Dissertation, p.14

Built form across the four cases

Qualitative readings from the dissertation — not scores

  • New Administrative Capital

    Near-identical glass towers and blocks on wide boulevards; isolated objects composed for the distant view.

  • Zamalek

    Historic apartment buildings with balconies and ornament; detailing is being replaced in renovation.

  • West el Balad

    Downtown façades over commercial ground floors; storefronts becoming minimal and generic.

  • Ashwa’iyat

    Compact incremental blocks that grow storey by storey; some unfinished or semi-inhabited.

04

Architects’ perspectives

Five recurring themes from semi-structured interviews with practising architects in Cairo.

  1. 01

    Developer-led urbanism

    The dominant theme across the interviews: market-centred logic and developer control drive homogenisation, prioritising visual appeal, branding and financial efficiency over climatic, cultural and contextual design.

    Source: Dissertation, p.20

  2. 02

    Identity beyond façades

    Homogenisation reaches into social life: the decline of public space and street life through private compounds and gated development, and an identity that lives in the daily life a façade contains rather than in heritage façades alone.

    Source: Dissertation, p.20, 21

  3. 03

    Loss of climate responsiveness

    Sealed glass buildings disregard shading, courtyards and local materials; the climate intelligence shared by minarets, courtyards and arcades across eras has, in one architect’s words, fractured.

    Source: Dissertation, p.21

  4. 04

    Adaptive reuse and context

    Strategies that resist homogenisation: preserving historic exteriors while adapting interiors, and designing from site conditions, climate and human behaviour to encourage movement, interaction and belonging.

    Source: Dissertation, p.22

  5. 05

    Informal practices as carriers of identity

    The Ashwa’iyat are read not as failed attempts but as sites of organic identity, where murals and continued rituals such as hospitality keep core experiences alive alongside physical preservation.

    Source: Dissertation, p.22, 23

05

Insights and conclusion

  • “Across all four studies, homogenisation comes out as a complex process. It is controlled by economic ambition, developer control, and foreign architectural styles.”

    Dissertation, p.19

  • “These case studies demonstrate that architectural identity in Cairo is not vanishing overtime, but is being renovated bit by bit.”

    Dissertation, p.19

  • “Beyond façades and styles, identity is born in atmosphere, rituals, and interactions.”

    Dissertation, p.24

“Homogenisation is not a final outcome, but an ongoing back and forth discussion. Merely a negotiation between modernisation and memory. Cairo has the opportunity to change repetition into relevance and homogenisation into shared identity.”

Dissertation, p.25

These conclusions are the author’s qualitative analysis. The dissertation does not flatten its findings into a claim that modern architecture is bad: identity is shown changing through negotiation between development, memory, local practice and everyday life.

06

Limitations, ethics and sources

  • The research is limited by the number of interviews conducted. — Dissertation, p.11
  • Translation from Arabic to English may lose depth and intensity of meaning, and one interview was conducted by e-mail without discussion. — Dissertation, p.11
  • The selected case studies provide depth but do not represent all urban conditions within Greater Cairo, and time limited the extent of on-site observation. — Dissertation, p.11

The research followed the University of Reading’s ethical guidelines. Interviewees gave informed consent and chose to be named; drawings focused on public environments and architecture and avoided including individuals. — Dissertation, p.11
